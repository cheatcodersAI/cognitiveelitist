In 2005, David Foster Wallace gave a commencement speech that opened with a parable:

> There are these two young fish swimming along and they happen to meet an older fish swimming the other way, who nods at them and says "Morning, boys. How's the water?" And the two young fish swim on for a bit, and then eventually one of them looks over at the other and goes "What the hell is water?"

The point, Wallace explained, is that the most obvious, important realities are often the hardest to see and talk about. Not because they're hidden, but because they're *everywhere*.

In 1997—eight years before Wallace's speech—James Dale Davidson and Lord William Rees-Mogg published *The Sovereign Individual*. Their prediction: the Information Age would fundamentally restructure human society. Digital technology would shift power from institutions to individuals. Geography would matter less. Borders would become porous. The capable would gain unprecedented freedom; the dependent would face unprecedented uncertainty.

They were describing water.

## We're Swimming In It

Twenty years later, we're living in the transformation they predicted—and most people still don't see it.

**Remote work isn't a pandemic blip.** It's the early stage of location becoming irrelevant for knowledge work. The capable can work from anywhere, serve clients anywhere, live anywhere. This was unthinkable a generation ago. Now it's Tuesday.

**Digital money isn't speculation.** It's the unbundling of currency from state control. Whether Bitcoin specifically succeeds is almost beside the point—the genie is out of the bottle. Money is becoming a technology choice, not a geographic default.

**AI isn't a tool.** It's a force multiplier that makes individuals more powerful than teams used to be. One person with the right tools can now accomplish what required an organization. Institutions that exist primarily to coordinate human effort are becoming obsolete.

**Credentialism is collapsing.** The degree used to be the signal. Now the portfolio, the track record, the verifiable output is the signal. You can learn anything. You can prove anything. The gatekeepers are losing their gates.

This is water.

## The Fish Who See

Wallace's speech wasn't just about awareness—it was about *choice*. The choice of what to pay attention to. The choice of how to construct meaning from the relentless flood of experience.

> The really important kind of freedom involves attention and awareness and discipline, and being able truly to care about other people and to sacrifice for them over and over in the mundane little ways every day.

The Sovereign Individual makes a colder observation: some people will recognize the shift and adapt. They will build skills that transcend borders. They will accumulate assets that can't be easily seized or inflated away. They will construct lives that don't depend on institutional permission.

Others will not. They will cling to the world they knew. They will demand that institutions protect them from change. They will be confused and angry when the institutions can't deliver.

Both perspectives point to the same truth: **awareness is the differentiator**.

The fish who notices the water can make choices about it. The fish who doesn't is simply carried by the current.

## What Does This Mean For Us?

If you're reading this site, you're probably already one of the fish who senses something.

Maybe you're neurodivergent and the normal paths never quite fit. Maybe you've always felt like the rules were written for someone else. Maybe you've been building skills, accumulating knowledge, positioning yourself without quite knowing why.

Here's the frame: **the transformation is real, and it favors the prepared.**

Not the credentialed. Not the connected. Not the compliant. The prepared.

The person who can produce value independent of geography and institution. The person who can learn faster than job descriptions change. The person who owns their tools and their data. The person who can see the water.

Wallace warned against the "default setting"—the unconscious, automatic way of moving through life where you're the center of everything and other people are obstacles. The Sovereign Individual warns against a different default: assuming the structures that shaped the 20th century will continue to shape the 21st.

Both warnings point to the same imperative: **wake up**.

See the water. Choose what to pay attention to. Build accordingly.

## The Uncomfortable Part

There's something uncomfortable about both of these texts.

Wallace's speech, for all its wisdom, ends in shadow. He took his own life three years after giving it. The awareness he described—the moment-to-moment consciousness of choice—is exhausting. The water is everywhere, all the time. You can't unsee it.

The Sovereign Individual, for all its prescience, has a coldness to it. Not everyone will thrive in the world it describes. The authors don't pretend otherwise. The capable will compound their advantages. The less capable will struggle. This isn't a moral judgment—it's an observation about how systems work when the friction of geography and institution dissolves.

We're not here to pretend this is easy or fair.

We're here because seeing is better than not seeing. Because building for the world that's emerging beats clinging to the world that's fading. Because even if the water is cold and the currents are strong, the fish who knows it's swimming has an advantage over the fish who thinks it's just… existing.

## Start Swimming

The transformation is underway. You're in it whether you recognize it or not.

The question is what you do next.

Learn skills that transcend borders. Build assets that can't be taken. Own your tools. Own your data. Position yourself for the world that's coming, not the world that was.

Most people won't. They'll keep asking why the jobs are different, why the money feels unstable, why the old paths don't lead where they used to.

They can't see the water.

You can.
